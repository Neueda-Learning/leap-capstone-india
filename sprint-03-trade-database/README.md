# Sprint 3: the trade database

Every other component in this platform is a way of getting something into this
database or out of it. The Trade REST API in Sprint 6 writes orders to it. The
Trade Executor in Sprint 7 updates them and moves cash and holdings in one
transaction. The pipeline in Sprint 7 extracts from it. The Angular blotter in
Sprint 9 renders what it returns. This is the platform's memory, and it is the
only part of the system whose contents are the legal record of what a customer
asked for and what happened next.

That makes two failure modes expensive. A schema that allows an impossible row
produces a record somebody has to correct by hand later: a negative holding, an
order against an account that was closed, two copies of the same submitted
order. A schema that is reshaped in Sprint 7 forces a rewrite of every mapper,
every query and every fixture written against it. You are designing for both:
correctness enforced by the database rather than hoped for in application code,
and a shape that survives the rest of the programme.

You have written SQL against somebody else's schema before. The new thing is
owning the design, and being held to it for eight more sprints by services you
have not written yet. There is no schema file in `contracts/`, deliberately,
because the modelling is the assessment. What follows is the domain. Turn it
into a model.

The build is scoped for the embedded capstone day and the evenings around it.
Most of the value is in the hour before the first migration, spent arguing
about entities on a whiteboard, so do not spend that hour typing.

## The domain

A retail brokerage runs the platform. Its customers hold cash with the firm,
buy and sell instruments through it, and expect to see what they own and what
they have done.

### Accounts

A customer trades through an account. An account holds a cash balance in a
single currency, carries the name of the holder, and is identified two ways:
by an internal key that the rest of the platform passes around, and by a
customer-facing reference that support staff and statements quote. Those two
are not the same thing and are not interchangeable.

An account is in exactly one of three states. `ACTIVE` accounts trade.
`SUSPENDED` accounts are frozen by compliance or by an unpaid balance: they can
be read, they cannot trade, and the suspension is reversible. `CLOSED`
accounts are finished. A closed account never trades again, but its history
must remain readable for years, so closing an account cannot mean deleting it.

Concurrency matters on this entity more than any other. Two orders arriving at
once for the same account both read the balance, both decide there is enough
cash, and both spend it. Your model needs a way for the second writer to
discover that it lost, rather than overwriting the first.

### Instruments

An instrument is something tradable: an equity, an exchange traded fund, a
currency pair, a crypto pair. Each has a symbol in the scheme the market-data
API uses, a display name, an asset class and a currency of quotation. Symbols
are externally assigned and stable, and the market-data API is the authority on
them.

An instrument can stop being tradable without ceasing to exist. A delisted name
still appears in years of order history, and somebody may still hold it, so
removing the row is not an option. The model has to refuse new orders in an
instrument while still resolving old ones.

### Orders

An order is a customer's instruction to buy or sell a quantity of one
instrument at a stated price, placed against one account. It is recorded when
it is received, before anyone knows whether it will succeed, because the
instruction itself is the thing the firm is legally on the hook for.

An order starts at `NEW`. From there it reaches exactly one terminal state:
`FILLED` when it executes, `REJECTED` when it cannot, `CANCELLED` when the
customer withdraws it before execution. Terminal means terminal. A filled order
is never reopened, and no state means half filled: the Trade Executor you build
in Sprint 7 fills in full or rejects.

Rejected orders are kept. A customer who was refused four times last Tuesday is
a compliance question, and the answer has to be in the database. Orders
accumulate without limit, are read far more often than they are written, are
never deleted, and are the table every later sprint queries hardest.

### The idempotency key

A customer taps Buy. The response is slow, the phone drops the connection, the
app retries. Without a defence, the firm has bought twice.

The defence is a key generated by the client, sent with the order, and unique
across all orders forever. A retry carries the key of the request it is
retrying, so the second attempt is recognisable as the same instruction rather
than a new one.

Where you enforce that uniqueness decides whether it works. Reading first to
see whether the key exists, then inserting, is not enforcement: two concurrent
requests both read nothing and both insert. The database has to be the thing
that refuses, under a constraint, so that one of the two writers fails no
matter how the requests interleave. Sprint 6 depends on that failure being
detectable, so design for it now.

### Positions

A position is what an account currently holds in one instrument: a quantity and
the average price paid for it. An account holds each instrument at most once,
and quantities never go negative because short selling is out of scope.

Positions are derived state. Each one could in principle be rebuilt by
replaying the filled orders for that account, and a team that can demonstrate
that reconciliation understands its own model. They are stored anyway, because
the dashboard cannot replay an order history on every page load.

### Money

Cash balances and prices are exact quantities. A binary floating point balance
cannot represent 0.10 and drifts by a few paise over a few thousand trades,
which is the kind of defect that surfaces in a reconciliation six months later.
Choose types that represent money exactly and apply them consistently.

## Migrations and the apply command

Schema changes are migrations, not edits. Each one is a `.sql` file in
`migrations/`, numbered from `001_`, applied in filename order. Three digits
leave room for the rest of the programme, and the number is the order, so the
order is part of the schema.

Until your first design review, editing a migration in place is fine: you are
still converging. After the review, and for the rest of the programme, an old
migration is immutable. Change the schema by adding `002_`, `003_` and so on.
The reason is not ceremony. From Sprint 6 your teammates have databases with
data in them, and an edited `001_` never runs again on any of them.

Applying all of that is one command, and writing it is part of this
deliverable. It has to:

- take a database that exists and is empty to fully migrated and seeded
- apply every file in `migrations/` in filename order
- load every seed file in `seed/` afterwards, in filename order
- read its target database name from the `TARGET_DATABASE` environment
  variable, falling back to `POSTGRES_DB` from the repository `.env`
- run without prompting, and exit non-zero the moment anything fails

A shell script around `psql`, a Python script, a Makefile target: any of them
is acceptable. Name whichever you wrote in `DESIGN.md`, as a path or command line
relative to this folder, and expect it to be run from this folder with nothing
set for it beyond `TARGET_DATABASE`. A command that works only after you have
exported three variables by hand fails there and will fail for your teammates
too.

Two things reliably go wrong. `psql` without `-v ON_ERROR_STOP=1` reports an
error and carries on, so a half-applied migration exits zero and looks fine.
And a command that applies the migrations but never reaches `seed/` leaves a
correct empty schema, which looks fine until Sprint 4 has nothing to plot.

Run the command often. A schema that only works because of something a
teammate typed into psql on Tuesday will not survive the showcase.

From Sprint 6 your stack has to come up with the schema already in place. Copy
your migration and seed loading into `infra/postgres/`, keeping the numbering,
and mount that folder into your Postgres container so a fresh start builds the
database unattended. See `infra/postgres/README.md` for how that mount
behaves.

## The queries your design has to serve

An index is not free. It is paid for on every insert and every update to the
columns it covers, and orders is the most write-heavy table you have. So no
index goes in without a query behind it, and these are the queries. Write them
out in SQL, run them against your loaded data, and read the plan with
`EXPLAIN ANALYZE` before and after you add the index.

| # | Query, in business terms | Who runs it |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | All open orders for one account, newest first | The blotter, on every dashboard load |
| 2 | The last 50 orders for one account in any state, newest first | The order history screen |
| 3 | Everything one account currently holds, with quantity and average cost | The portfolio panel, on every dashboard load |
| 4 | Every order created since a given timestamp, across all accounts | The nightly extract into the analytical store, Sprint 7 |
| 5 | Resolve an account from the customer-facing reference quoted on a call | Support, and the auth service at sign-in |
| 6 | For one account, every filled order oldest first, each with the running total of cash committed to date and its rank by value within its instrument | The monthly statement |

Justify at least three indexes against three of queries 1 to 5. A justification
names the query, states what the plan does without the index and with it, and
says what the index costs on write. "It seemed likely to help" is not a
justification.

Some of these need no new index, because a key you already declared serves
them. Write that down too, with the plan that proves it. It is a real finding
and it is the cheapest index you will ever add. It does not count towards the
three.

Query 6 is different. It exists because a window function or a common table
expression is the honest way to answer it, and because a model that makes it
awkward is telling you something. It earns no index. Write it, keep it with the
others, and bring it to the review.

## The ER diagram

One diagram, committed to `design/`, showing every entity, its attributes, its
keys and the cardinality and optionality of every relationship. Two formats are
acceptable:

- Mermaid in a markdown file, `design/er-diagram.md`, using an `erDiagram`
  block. This is the better choice, because it diffs in review and it cannot
  drift out of the repository.
- An exported image, `design/er-diagram.png` or `.svg`, from whatever tool you
  drew it in. Commit the export, not a link to a cloud document that your
  instructor cannot open in the review.

Every member of the team walks the whole diagram unaided in the review. Not the
part they wrote. The whole diagram, including why a relationship is optional
and where a cardinality came from.

## The seed data

You write the fixtures. `seed/README.md` states what they have to cover:
accounts in all three states, an account with almost no cash, an instrument
that no longer trades, orders in all four lifecycle states, and holdings that
reconcile against the filled orders that produced them. Every one of those
exists to make an error path reachable somewhere later in the programme, so
read that file before you design rather than after.

The requirements there are business facts, not a schema in disguise. How many
tables the data belongs in, which value becomes a key, and what identifies an
account internally are your decisions. Writing the inserts is where those
decisions first get tested. An order carries the customer-facing account
reference a customer would quote on a call, and resolving that to whatever your
schema uses internally is exactly the mapping the Trade REST API performs in
Sprint 6.

## Historical trade data

The operational tables answer "what is true right now". The desk also asks
"what did we trade last quarter, by instrument, by week". Those are different
questions with different access patterns, and Sprint 4 builds a separate
analytical store for the second kind.

This sprint delivers the reasoning, not the rows. Commit `DESIGN.md` in this
folder covering:

- what you would retain about an executed trade beyond what the order carries,
  and at what grain
- how those structures are populated, and by which component
- how the Sprint 7 extract pulls from them incrementally rather than scanning
  the whole history every night
- how the design behaves at a hundred times the volume: partitioning, archival,
  retention, or a defensible argument that none of it is needed yet
- what it costs, on write and in operational complexity

Two pages is enough. It is discussed at the design review, and a decision you
can defend beats a diagram nobody can explain.

## Acceptance criteria

1. The schema is in third normal form, with no redundant or transitive
   dependencies, and any deliberate denormalisation is documented with its
   reason.
2. The schema is version controlled as migration files, applied in a stable
   order, and the database can be rebuilt from those files alone.
3. Foreign keys and check constraints are present and enforce the domain rules,
   including a unique constraint on the order idempotency key, at least two
   foreign keys, and at least three check constraints, one of them covering
   account state.
4. At least three indexes exist and each is justified by one of the named
   queries.
5. Seed data covering the states named in `seed/README.md` loads with one
   command, and the six named queries return sensible answers against it.
6. `DESIGN.md` covers the historical trade data design, including how it is
   populated, how it is queried, and how it behaves as it grows.
7. An ER diagram is committed, and every team member can walk it unaided.

Two of those constraints have to be demonstrated rather than described. Write a
statement that inserts the same order twice under one idempotency key and is
refused by the database with SQLSTATE `23505`, unique violation. Write a second
that inserts a row referencing a parent that does not exist and is refused with
SQLSTATE `23503`, foreign key violation. Run both against your loaded database in
front of your instructor. Reading the constraint out of a migration file is not
the same as watching the database refuse the row.

One further criterion is worth more than the rest.
The model has to be the one the platform needs, not one that fits only this
week. From Sprint 5 your Java entities are built against it, in Sprint 6 your
mappers are written against it, and in Sprint 7 two services write to it
concurrently. Read `contracts/trade-api.yaml` while you design. It is the
contract the Sprint 6 service satisfies out of this database, and a schema that
cannot serve it is the wrong schema. Reading it is research, not shortcutting.

## Evaluation

This sprint contributes 7 marks to the 100-mark Capstone assessment. The domain
brief, the contracts and the seed coverage requirements in `seed/README.md` are
inputs. Everything else, including the seed data itself, is the team's work.
Marks are awarded for the database design, implementation and evidence the team
produces.

| Criterion | Marks |
|---|---:|
| ER model, entity relationships and normalisation decisions | 2 |
| DDL, keys, foreign keys, checks and required core tables | 2 |
| Index justifications and historical trade data design | 1 |
| Repeatable migrations, complete seed loading and review evidence | 2 |
| **Sprint total** | **7** |

## The design review

Satisfying every countable criterion above is necessary and it is not
sufficient. Nothing mechanical can tell you whether your schema is in third
normal form, whether an index is justified or merely present, whether your
historical design is the right one, or whether your team can explain any of it.
Your instructor assesses that by reading the schema and the design notes against
the criteria, and the review is the assessment. A team can tick every box with a
model that is quietly wrong in a way that costs them a fortnight in Sprint 7.

Bring to the review: the ER diagram, your migrations, your apply command run
against an empty database, the six queries with their plans, your index
justifications, `DESIGN.md`, the two rejection statements above, and the answer
to "why is it this way and not the other way" for the three decisions you argued
about the longest.
